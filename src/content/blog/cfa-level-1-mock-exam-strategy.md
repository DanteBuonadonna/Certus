# CFA Level 1 Mock Exam Strategy: How Many and When

Take three to five full-length, timed mock exams, and put the first one roughly six weeks before exam day — not in the final week. The number matters far less than the review: a mock you spend three hours dissecting is worth more than three mocks you score and forget.

Most candidates get this backwards. They save mocks for the end as a kind of final verdict, run out of time, take one or two in a panic, and never learn anything from them. A mock is a diagnostic tool, not a report card. Here's how to use it that way.

## What a Level 1 mock actually has to simulate

The real exam is 180 multiple-choice questions split into two sessions of 135 minutes each — 90 questions per session, three answer choices (A, B, C), all questions weighted equally, and no penalty for a wrong answer. That works out to about 90 seconds per question.

If your "mock" is 60 untimed questions in a browser tab with your notes open, it isn't a mock. It's a quiz. The three things a mock must reproduce:

1. **Full length.** Question 150 feels nothing like question 15. Fatigue is a skill you have to train.
2. **Real pacing.** 90 seconds a question, clock running, no pausing to look things up.
3. **The real topic mix.** CFA Institute states that its official mocks use the same topic weights and difficulty as the actual exam. A mock that's 40% ethics teaches you the wrong thing about where your time goes.

CFA Institute posts mock exams inside the Learning Ecosystem for registered candidates, and sells an optional Practice Pack add-on that gives Level I candidates 1,000 extra questions and five additional official mock exams. Those official mocks are the closest thing to the real item style, so use them — but they aren't the only reps you need, and you shouldn't burn them all early.

## The schedule: work backward from exam day

Assume a normal Level 1 prep window. Here's a mock schedule that works whether you're on a 3-month plan or a 6-month one, because it's anchored to the exam, not the start date.

**Week −6: Mock #1 (the honest baseline).** Take it before you feel ready. This is the single most-skipped and most valuable mock. You will score badly. That's the point — you now have six weeks and a ranked list of what's broken, instead of a vague feeling that you should "review more FRA."

**Week −4: Mock #2.** After you've attacked the two or three weakest topics from Mock #1. This one tells you whether your fixes actually took.

**Week −2: Mock #3.** Full dress rehearsal. Same start time as your real exam appointment, same calculator, same breakfast, no phone. If you've been doing mocks on a laptop in bed, this is where you find out that sitting upright for four and a half hours is its own problem.

**Week −1: Mock #4 (optional).** Only if you have the energy to review it properly. A mock you don't review is just an expensive way to get tired.

**Final 3 days: no mocks.** Light review of your error log, formulas, and ethics. Cramming a mock 48 hours out mostly buys you anxiety.

If you have less runway than that, compress — but never drop below two full-length timed mocks, and never let the last one be the first one.

## How to review a mock (this is where the score comes from)

Budget as long for review as for the mock itself. Two to three hours per mock, minimum. For every question you didn't get cleanly right, sort it into one of four buckets:

- **Didn't know it.** A genuine content gap. Goes back into the study queue.
- **Knew it, misread it.** You solved for the wrong thing, missed a "least likely," or used annual instead of semiannual. This is a process error, and process errors are the cheapest points on the whole exam to recover.
- **Knew it, ran out of time.** A pacing problem, not a knowledge problem. Different fix entirely.
- **Guessed right.** Mark these. A lucky guess is a future wrong answer wearing a disguise.

That fourth bucket is why raw scores lie. If you scored 68% but guessed your way into 9 of those, your real command of the material is closer to 63%. Track it honestly.

Keep a running error log — one line per miss: topic, what went wrong, the correction. By week −2, that log is your entire study plan. It beats re-reading the curriculum by a wide margin, because it's a list of things you have personally, provably gotten wrong.

## What score should you be hitting?

CFA Institute doesn't publish the minimum passing score, and it sets the MPS separately for every exam window. Anyone quoting you an exact number is estimating. Third-party estimates for Level 1 tend to land in the high 60s, but treat that as folklore, not fact.

The useful way to read a mock score is as a trend, not a threshold. Scoring in the 60s six weeks out with a clear upward slope is a much better sign than a flat 70 that hasn't moved in a month. And for context on why the bar feels high: recent Level 1 pass rates have run around 39–45% per window, against a long-run average near 40%. The exam is genuinely selective, and mocks are how you find out where you stand before it's expensive to find out.

One more note on scoring: since February 2025, CFA Institute's score report includes a scale score showing how your overall result compared to the MPS, along with topic-level performance. Your own mock reviews should mirror that structure — always look at performance *by topic*, never just the headline percentage.

## The short version

Three to five full-length timed mocks. First one six weeks out, before you feel ready. Review each for as long as you took it, sorted into knowledge gaps, process errors, pacing problems, and lucky guesses. Watch the trend, not the threshold. Stop taking mocks three days before exam day.
