# How Many Hours to Study for CFA Level 3?

Plan on **300 to 400 hours** for CFA Level 3. CFA Institute says successful candidates report studying an average of over 300 hours for each level, and its 2019 candidate survey put the Level III average at about 344 hours — the highest of the three levels.

The total is only slightly higher than Level I or II. What changes is where the hours have to go. Level III is the one level where reading time converts worst into exam points, because roughly half the exam is written answers, and writing them well is a skill you only build by doing it.

## What you're preparing for

The format drives the hour budget, so it's worth knowing exactly what you'll face. Per CFA Institute's Level III exam page:

- **4 hours and 24 minutes**, split into two 2-hour-12-minute sessions with an optional break.
- **11 item sets and 11 essay (constructed response) sets**, each worth 12 points. Twenty are scored; one item set and one essay set are unscored trial questions you can't identify.
- Each item set is a vignette with **four multiple-choice questions** worth 3 points each.
- Each essay set has a variable number of questions, and **points per question aren't disclosed**.
- Each session has either 6 item sets and 5 essay sets, or the reverse.

That works out to about **12 minutes per 12-point set**. On the item sets that's comfortable. On essay sets it's tight, because you have to read the vignette, work out what the command word ("Determine," "Justify," "Discuss") is asking for, and type a response.

Two rules from CFA Institute's guidance change how you should practice. First, if a question asks for two reasons, only the first two you give are graded. Extra answers don't earn credit. Second, a correct number typed into the essay box by itself gets full credit on a calculation, but showing your steps is what gives you a chance at partial credit if the final number is wrong.

## Where the content hours go

Level III is portfolio management from top to bottom. The current core topic weights are:

| Topic | Exam weight |
| --- | --- |
| Asset Allocation | 15–20% |
| Portfolio Construction | 15–20% |
| Derivatives and Risk Management | 10–15% |
| Ethical and Professional Standards | 10–15% |
| Performance Measurement | 5–10% |
| Your pathway (Portfolio Management, Private Markets, or Private Wealth) | 30–35% |

Two things stand out. Your **pathway is the single biggest block on the exam**, worth as much as a third of your score, so it deserves a third of your content hours, not leftovers. And asset allocation plus portfolio construction together can be up to 40% of the exam. If you haven't picked a pathway yet, our guide to [choosing a CFA Level 3 pathway](/blog/cfa-level-3-pathways-which-to-choose) walks through the trade-offs.

## A realistic 350-hour budget

Here's a split that matches how the exam is actually scored, built around about 14 hours a week for six months:

**First pass through the curriculum: about 150 hours.** That's less than you'd spend at Level I or II, on purpose. A lot of Level III (IPS construction, asset allocation, derivatives strategies) is applied rather than new, and candidates who spend 250 hours on a first read run out of time for the part that matters most. Weight the hours by the table above, with the pathway getting roughly a third.

**Essay practice: about 100 hours.** This is the block most candidates underfund. Work CFA Institute's past constructed-response questions and the essay practice in the Learning Ecosystem, and grade yourself honestly against the guideline answers. You're training three specific habits: answering exactly the number of points asked for, leading with the answer ("Portfolio B" before the reasoning), and keeping justifications to a sentence or two. Long, careful paragraphs are a time trap at 12 minutes a set.

**Item sets and topic review: about 50 hours.** Timed vignettes by topic, with the same goal as Level II: about three minutes per question, including reading.

**Full mocks and review: about 50 hours.** At least three full-length, timed mocks, each followed by a review that takes longer than the mock itself. A 4.5-hour mock plus a proper review is most of a weekend, which is why mocks need to be on the calendar from week one.

**Plus the Practical Skills Module.** You must complete one PSM at each level to receive your result, and it can't be one you already did at Level I or II. It's a few evenings of work. Do it early.

If you're coming straight off a Level II pass, you can probably trim the first pass toward 120 hours. If it's been a year or more since Level II, or ethics at Level I and II felt shaky, budget closer to 400 total.

## What the pass rates say about the hours

Level III has the highest pass rate of the three levels, but it's still close to a coin flip: **50% in February 2026**, with a 10-year average of 51% according to CFA Institute. Our [CFA Level 3 pass rate breakdown](/blog/cfa-level-3-pass-rate) has every recent window.

The more useful number is the gap between candidate groups. In the February 2026 window, first-time Level III candidates passed at 59%, compared with 34% for candidates who had at least one deferral behind them. Part of that is who chooses to defer, but losing momentum costs real points at this level. If you're torn between taking the next window on a compressed plan or pushing back six months, the data favors taking it.

## Timing your start

Level III is offered only twice a year, in February and August. The February 2027 window runs February 18–21, and the final registration deadline is **November 5, 2026**. Starting this week gives you about 19 weeks, or roughly 18 hours a week to reach 350 hours. That's doable if you've just passed Level II, but tight for anyone working long hours.

The August 2027 window (August 11–14) has a final registration deadline of April 29, 2027. Starting by late February gives you the standard six-month runway at about 14 hours a week. Our [CFA exam registration deadlines](/blog/cfa-exam-registration-deadlines) post has every date and fee.

## How to tell if your hours are working

Track two things instead of hours logged:

1. **Your self-graded essay score by topic.** If you're consistently losing points on one topic, check whether it's a knowledge gap or a format problem. Often it's the format: you gave three reasons when the question asked for two, or you buried the answer in the last sentence.
2. **Whether you finish mocks on time.** Running out of time at Level III almost always comes from essay length, not from slow math. If you're finishing with ten minutes or less to spare, shorten your answers before you add more reading.
