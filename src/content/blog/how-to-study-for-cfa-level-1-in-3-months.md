# How to Study for CFA Level 1 in 3 Months

Yes, three months is enough to pass CFA Level 1 — but only if you run it like a triage operation, not a leisurely read-through. The CFA Institute recommends around 300 hours per level. Over 13 weeks that's about 23 hours a week, or a little over 3 hours a day. That's demanding but doable, especially if you're disciplined about *what* you study, not just how long.

Here's the plan that actually works when the clock is short.

## The one rule that makes 3 months possible

Start doing practice questions in week one — not week eight. The most common way people fail on a compressed timeline is spending the first six weeks reading, feeling productive, and only then discovering they can't *apply* any of it under a clock. Reading builds recognition; questions build recall. On a three-month runway you cannot afford the gap between the two.

So flip the usual ratio. Read a topic just enough to understand it, then immediately drill questions on it and let your misses tell you what to re-read. The questions are the curriculum; the readings are the reference manual you consult after a question shows you what you don't know.

## The 13-week split

**Weeks 1–7 — Learn + drill (the bulk).** Move through all ten topics once, doing questions immediately after each. Prioritize by exam weight — don't spend a week perfecting a 6% topic while a 13% topic stays shaky. Front-load the four that carry the most points:

- **Financial Statement Analysis (~11–14%)** — the biggest, most mechanical section. Master how the three statements link and the indirect-method cash flow statement.
- **Ethics (~15–20%)** — the single highest weight *and* the borderline tiebreaker. Start it in week one and do Ethics questions every week. It's application, not memorization.
- **Equity and Fixed Income (~11–14% each)** — both lean on time-value-of-money, so nail Quant early and these get easier.

**Weeks 8–10 — Drill hard by topic.** Work a large question bank, keeping an error log: every miss, and *why* you missed it (didn't know it, misread it, calculator slip, or ran out of time). Roughly half your misses won't be knowledge gaps at all — they'll be misreads and pacing. Fixing those is fast and worth more points than another read-through.

**Weeks 11–13 — Mock and refine.** Stop learning new material. Take full-length, timed mocks under real conditions — no pausing, no phone, no looking things up. Review each mock harder than you took it, and study only what the error log tells you. Do a heavy Ethics pass in the final week; it retains best when it's fresh.

## Find your starting point first

Before you build the schedule, find out where you actually stand — it changes everything about how you spend these 90 days. If you're already strong in FSA but weak in Derivatives, your plan looks completely different from someone in the reverse. Guessing wastes the one resource you don't have: time.

## The 90-second habit

Level 1 gives you about 90 seconds per question. On a short timeline, pacing is a skill you have to train deliberately, so use a timer from week one. The goal isn't answering fast — it's recognizing at second 20 that a question will take four minutes, flagging it, and moving on without ego. A question you skip costs one point; a question you stubbornly grind costs that point plus the three easy ones you never reach.

## A realistic weekly rhythm

- **Weekdays:** 2–3 hours — one topic's readings plus a question set, or pure question drilling in the later weeks.
- **One weekday:** Ethics questions, every single week, no exceptions.
- **Weekends:** 4–5 hours — catch up, review your error log, and (from week 8) take timed sets that grow into full mocks.

If you fall behind — and you will, some weeks — don't try to "make up" reading. Triage: protect Ethics and FSA, protect the heavy topics you're already good at, and let a light section like Alternatives slide if you truly must.
