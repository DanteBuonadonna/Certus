# Failed CFA Level 1? What to Do Next

If you failed CFA Level 1, you can retake it — and for most August 2026 candidates, the realistic next shot is the February 2027 or May 2027 window. Before you pick a date, spend an evening with your score report: it tells you exactly how far you were from passing and which topics cost you, and that should decide both *when* you retake and *how* you study.

You're also in large company. CFA Institute reported that 43% of the 28,145 candidates who took Level I in August 2026 passed — meaning more than half did not. That was an above-average window. Failing Level 1 is common; failing it twice for the same reasons is the thing to avoid.

## Step 1: Read your score report properly

Your result isn't just "fail." According to CFA Institute, every candidate gets a topic-level performance breakdown and an **overall scale score** that shows where you landed relative to the Minimum Passing Score (MPS). CFA Institute doesn't publish the MPS as a percentage, and it doesn't release your answers to individual questions, so the report is the best diagnostic you'll get.

Look at it in two layers:

**How far off were you?** If your scale score sat just under the MPS line, your problem is probably execution — timing, careless errors, a couple of weak topics. If you were well below it, you likely have a coverage problem: large parts of the curriculum never stuck. These call for different plans (more on that below).

**Where did the points go?** Rank your topics from weakest to strongest, then weight that list by how much each topic is worth on the exam. A weak result in a 6–9% topic hurts far less than a weak result in an 11–14% topic. Our breakdown of [CFA Level 1 topic weights](/blog/cfa-level-1-topic-weights-explained) covers the math.

Save or screenshot the report now. CFA Institute keeps results available for about a year, and after that a verification letter shows only pass or fail — not the topic breakdown.

## Step 2: Know the retake rules

CFA Institute's eligibility policy is short, but it shapes your whole timeline:

- You can take a given level **at most twice per calendar year**.
- Attempts can't be in **consecutive testing windows** or in windows **less than six months apart**.
- You get **six total attempts per level**. A partially completed exam (say, you only finish one session) still counts.

Level I is offered in February, May, August, and November. If you took it in August 2026, November is off the table (consecutive window). February 2027 is the earliest window most August candidates can target; the registration system enforces the six-month rule, so confirm your eligibility when you register.

## Step 3: Pick the right window — February or May 2027?

Here are the two realistic options for an August 2026 candidate:

| Window | Level I test dates | Key registration deadline | Study runway from today |
|---|---|---|---|
| February 2027 | Feb 22–28, 2027 | Final deadline Nov 5, 2026 (schedule by Nov 10) | ~21 weeks |
| May 2027 | May 11–17, 2027 | Early-bird deadline Oct 14, 2026 | ~32 weeks |

Registering early for May costs less than registering for February at the standard fee, since February's early-bird window has already closed. Our [CFA exam registration deadlines](/blog/cfa-exam-registration-deadlines) guide has the full fee and date table.

A simple rule of thumb:

- **Close miss + you still remember most of the material → February.** Momentum matters. Your knowledge decays every month you wait, and five months is enough to fix a few topics and sharpen timing.
- **Well below the line, or burned out → May.** Rushing a second attempt with the same gaps just produces the same result. The extra runway lets you rebuild weak topics properly.

One more thing to weigh: starting with the February 2027 exam cycle, CFA Institute is **eliminating paid deferrals**. Under the old rules you could pay to push your exam back. From February 2027 on, if you can't take your scheduled exam, you forfeit the registration fee and have to re-register. In other words, pick a window you'll actually show up for. That matters more than it sounds. In the August 2026 window, first-time candidates passed at 49%, while candidates testing after at least one deferral passed at just 29%.

Also note that the curriculum shifts for 2027. CFA Institute's published 2027 Level I outline lowers Ethics to 10–15% (from 15–20% in 2026) and raises Quantitative Methods to 11–14% (from 6–9%). If you're retaking in 2027, study from the 2027 materials, not your old notes.

## Step 4: Diagnose *why* you failed

Your score report shows *where* you lost points. You also need to be honest about *why*. The common causes fall into a few buckets:

**You read more than you practiced.** This is the most common one. Reading the curriculum feels productive, but the exam doesn't test whether you recognize an idea. It tests whether you can apply it in about 90 seconds against three plausible answer choices. If most of your hours went into reading and videos, flip the ratio this time.

**You ran out of time.** Level I is 180 questions in two 135-minute sessions — CFA Institute suggests roughly 90 seconds per question. If you guessed through the last 15 questions of a session, that's a pacing problem, and it's very fixable with timed practice.

**You skipped full mocks, or took them too late.** A mock taken the week before the exam is a reality check with no time left to act on it. Mocks need to happen early enough to change your plan. Our [mock exam strategy guide](/blog/cfa-level-1-mock-exam-strategy) covers how many to take and when.

**You under-weighted the big topics.** Some candidates pour hours into topics they find interesting and coast on Financial Statement Analysis, Fixed Income, and Equity — which together make up a large share of the exam.

**You simply didn't put in the hours.** CFA Institute says successful candidates report spending over 300 hours on average preparing for each level. If you were at 150, the fix isn't a new strategy. It's more time.

## Step 5: Build a retake plan that's actually different

Whatever caused the first result, the second attempt has to look different. A solid structure:

1. **Weeks 1–2: Take a full timed mock right away.** Before you reread anything. This gives you a fresh baseline and shows what you've already forgotten.
2. **Middle phase: Attack weak, high-weight topics first.** Use your score report and that baseline mock to build a ranked list. Relearn with a practice-first loop: do questions, review every miss, then read *only* the section that explains the miss.
3. **Keep strong topics warm, not polished.** Short spaced reviews and flashcards are enough to stop decay. Don't re-study what you already know just because it feels good.
4. **Final 4–6 weeks: Full mocks under real conditions.** Two sessions, 135 minutes each, calculator only. Review each one as carefully as you took it.

If you're working full time, the practice-first approach is also the most time-efficient one. Short daily question sets beat a weekend spent rereading 80 pages.
