# Can You Self-Study for the CFA?

Yes — and not as a workaround. CFA Institute describes the CFA Program in its own press releases as "a rigorous self-study program," and its program page lists it as self-paced online learning with materials included. There are no required lectures, no attendance, no classroom component. Every charterholder alive got there by teaching themselves the curriculum.

So the real question isn't *can* you self-study. It's whether you can self-study **well**, and what a paid prep course actually adds that you can't build yourself for a fraction of the price.

## What your registration already buys you

Before you spend a dollar on prep, know what you already have. Registered candidates get access to the Learning Ecosystem (LES), which includes:

- The full official curriculum for your level
- 1,000+ practice questions
- Two official mock exams, released roughly 60 days before the exam window
- A study planner, curriculum-based flashcards, and Practical Skills Modules

That's a complete self-study package written by the same people who write the exam. There's also an optional paid add-on called the Practice Pack — at Level I it adds 1,000 more questions, immediate access to five official mocks, and one Prometric-simulated mock. It's optional, not required.

A lot of candidates buy a $500 course and then never open the LES. That's backwards. Official questions are the closest thing to the real exam that exists.

## The number that actually matters: 300 hours

CFA Institute puts the study commitment at roughly 300 hours per level. That figure is self-reported and imperfect — it comes from candidates who volunteered the number, which skews toward people who passed — but it's the right order of magnitude and it's the planning input that matters.

Do the arithmetic before you register, not after:

- **6 months** → ~26 weeks → **11–12 hours a week**
- **5 months** → ~22 weeks → **13–14 hours a week**
- **4 months** → ~17 weeks → **17–18 hours a week**
- **3 months** → ~13 weeks → **23 hours a week** (doable, brutal alongside a full-time job)

If you can't point to which 12 hours in your week those are — actual named blocks, not "evenings" — the plan isn't real yet. This is the single most common failure mode in self-study, and no prep course fixes it for you either.

## The pass rates, in context

For the May 2026 Level I exam, 39% of the 31,566 candidates worldwide passed. CFA Institute noted that's below recent cycles but close to Level I's long-term average of about 40%. February 2026 came in at 45%.

One split inside that data is worth internalizing: first-time candidates passed at 45%, while candidates taking the exam after at least one deferral passed at 25%. Deferring roughly halves your odds — not because those candidates are less capable, but because momentum is most of this. Pick a date you can actually hit and keep it.

## What a prep course really sells — and how to replicate it

Strip the marketing away and a $349–$1,499 course is selling four things. Each one has a cheap substitute.

**1. A schedule.** The most valuable part of most courses is that someone else decided what you study in week 7. You can build this yourself in an hour: take the [topic weights](/blog/cfa-level-1-topic-weights-explained), allocate your 300 hours in proportion, and put it in a calendar. Weight-proportional allocation is the whole trick — Ethics and FRA deserve real time; Alternative Investments does not deserve three weeks.

**2. A second explanation.** When the curriculum's treatment of a topic doesn't land, you need someone explaining it differently. This is the one genuinely hard thing to replicate for free — but it's also worth far less than $1,000, since you only need it for a handful of topics, not the whole curriculum.

**3. Question volume.** Reading is not studying. You need thousands of reps, ideally spaced out, ideally mixed across topics rather than drilled one chapter at a time. Between the LES's 1,000+ questions and a cheap third-party bank, you can get there without a premium tier.

**4. Diagnosis.** Knowing *which* topics you're weak in, specifically enough to act on. A full-length timed mock does this better than any dashboard — which is why the mock is the study tool, not the final check.

## A self-study plan that holds up

1. **Register first, then plan.** A dated exam is what turns intention into a schedule.
2. **Allocate by topic weight.** Not by what's interesting. Not alphabetically.
3. **Front-load Ethics, then revisit it.** It's the largest single Level I topic and the one most people leave until it's too late to absorb. Read it early, then do Ethics questions weekly all the way through.
4. **Take a full timed mock early — like, uncomfortably early.** Most candidates wait until week 20 and discover a structural problem with three weeks left. Take one at the 40% mark to find out what you don't know while there's still time to fix it. Our [mock exam strategy post](/blog/cfa-level-1-mock-exam-strategy) covers how to review one properly.
5. **Do questions daily, in mixed topics.** Twenty minutes a day of interleaved review beats a four-hour Saturday cram, and the research on spacing is not close.
6. **Learn your calculator early.** Fumbling the BA II Plus costs real points. [Here's the setup and the shortcuts worth knowing](/blog/ba-ii-plus-calculator-tips-cfa).
7. **Save the last three weeks for mocks and weak areas only.** No new material.

## When self-study is genuinely a bad idea

Being honest about this: if you have no finance or accounting background at all and eight weeks on the clock, self-study isn't the problem — the timeline is. Move the date. And if you've failed twice using the same method, the answer isn't more hours of that method; it's changing what you do with the hours, usually by shifting from reading to questions.

Everything else — full-time job, non-finance degree, tight budget, no local study group — is a scheduling problem, not a reason to spend $1,000.
