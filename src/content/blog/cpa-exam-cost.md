# CPA Exam Cost in 2026: Every Fee, Explained

The CPA exam itself costs $1,074.36 in section fees as of July 4, 2026: four sections at $268.59 each, paid to NASBA. Add your state board's application fee (often around $100–$200, and some states charge more), and the bare-minimum cost to take all four sections once lands somewhere a little above $1,150, before retakes, an ethics exam, or a review course.

Those extras are where the real cost lives. Here's every piece, what you can't avoid, and where you can save.

## The fees you pay no matter what

Every CPA candidate pays two kinds of fees just to get in the door.

**1. State application fee.** You apply through your state board of accountancy (or NASBA, which handles applications for many states). This fee is set by the state and is non-refundable. Two real examples from state boards:

- **California:** $100 for first-time applicants, $50 for repeat applicants already qualified in California.
- **Virginia:** $120, non-refundable, due when you submit the online application.

Some states also charge a separate per-section registration fee on top of the NASBA section fee, so check your own board's fee page before you budget.

**2. NASBA section fees.** Once you're approved, you receive a payment coupon and pay the section fee for each section you want to take. As of July 4, 2026, that fee is **$268.59 per section**. (Texas, Arizona, Mississippi, and Guam switched to the new rate on August 1, 2026; before that, candidates there paid $265.57.)

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| One section (NASBA) | $268.59 |
| All four sections (NASBA) | $1,074.36 |
| California, first attempt at all four (app fee + sections) | $1,174.36 |
| Virginia, first attempt at all four (app fee + sections) | $1,194.36 |

Those two state totals are the floor: what you'd pay if you passed every section on your first try and your state required nothing else.

## The rule that quietly costs candidates money

Once you pay for a section, you receive a Notice to Schedule (NTS), and it has an expiration date. NASBA's policy is blunt: if your NTS expires before you take the section, or you miss your appointment, you can't reschedule, you don't get a refund, and you have to reapply and pay the fees again. Refunds or extensions are only possible through NASBA's exception-to-policy process for documented hardship, and they're never automatic.

The practical lesson: **only pay for a section when you're ready to schedule it.** Buying all four up front feels efficient, but it turns your study plan into a set of deadlines with $268.59 riding on each one.

## Retakes: the cost most people forget to budget

Every retake means paying the section fee again, plus whatever your state charges for a re-exam application. That matters because CPA pass rates are lower than most candidates expect. In the first half of 2026, AICPA data put FAR at about 43% and AUD around 49%, while REG and ISC were in the mid-60s and TCP near 80% (full breakdown in our post on [CPA exam pass rates by section](/blog/cpa-exam-pass-rates-by-section)).

In other words, failing one Core section once is common, not a disaster. A realistic budget sets aside the cost of at least one retake, about $270 in NASBA fees plus your state's re-exam fee, so a single bad day doesn't throw off your finances along with your schedule.

## The ethics exam

Many states require a separate ethics exam for licensure, most often the AICPA's self-study ethics course and exam, which typically costs a few hundred dollars depending on whether you're an AICPA member. Requirements vary a lot. California, for example, no longer uses the AICPA ethics exam for initial licensure. Treat this as a licensure cost rather than an exam cost, and confirm the rule with your state board before you buy anything.

## Review courses: the biggest line item

For most candidates, the review course costs more than every official fee combined. Becker, the best-known provider, lists its entry Advantage package at $2,499, and its higher tiers cost more. Other big-name courses are in the same four-figure range.

You don't need the most expensive package to pass, but you do need three things:

1. **Coverage of the current blueprint.** Since the 2024 CPA Evolution changes, the exam is three Core sections (AUD, FAR, REG) plus one Discipline (BAR, ISC, or TCP). Materials written for the old four-section format (with BEC) are out of date.
2. **Lots of practice questions and simulations.** In most sections, task-based simulations count for half your score. Reading alone won't get you there.
3. **A routine you'll actually keep.** Most candidates who run out of time don't lack material. They fall behind on consistency.

## A realistic total

Here's how the costs stack up for a typical candidate in a state like California or Virginia:

| Budget scenario | What's included | Approximate total |
|---|---|---|
| Bare minimum | State app fee + four sections, all passed first try | ~$1,175–$1,200 |
| Realistic, self-study | Above + one retake + low-cost study tools | ~$1,550–$1,900 |
| Big-name course | Bare minimum + a $2,499 review package + one retake | ~$3,950–$4,000+ |

The "realistic" row assumes one retake (about $270 plus any state re-exam fee) and roughly $100–$400 of study tools. Ethics exam and licensing fees come later and vary by state, so they're not included.

## How to keep your CPA exam cost down

- **Pay per section, not all at once.** You avoid forfeiting fees on an NTS you can't use.
- **Take sections in a smart order.** Pairing related material (for example REG and then TCP, which overlap heavily) means less re-learning. See our guide to [what order to take the CPA sections](/blog/cpa-exam-order-of-sections).
- **Check employer reimbursement.** Many accounting firms reimburse exam fees, review courses, or both. Ask before you pay out of pocket.
- **Don't pay for features you won't use.** Live classes and coaching are nice, but practice volume and consistency are what move your score.
- **Practice under real conditions before exam day.** A full-length timed practice run is the cheapest way to avoid a $270 retake.

## The bottom line

The CPA exam costs $1,074.36 in NASBA fees plus your state's application fee, so a little over $1,150 at minimum in many states. Most candidates end up spending somewhere between roughly $1,550 and $4,000+ once retakes and prep are counted, and the review course is the biggest variable. You control most of that number through how you buy sections and how you study.
