# How Many Hours to Study for CFA Level 2?

CFA Institute reports that successful candidates study **over 300 hours for each level** of the CFA Program, and its own 2019 candidate survey put the Level II average at roughly 328 hours. Plan for 300–400 hours spread over five to six months, and treat 350 as the number to aim at if you're working full time.

That range is wider than it looks, because the variable isn't intelligence — it's how much of Level I you actually retained. Level II is not a new syllabus bolted onto the old one. It re-tests the same ten topics at a depth that assumes you still remember the Level I mechanics cold. Candidates who passed Level I eight months ago and haven't touched a bond price since are the ones who need 400 hours. Candidates coming straight off a Level I pass with the formulas still live need closer to 300.

## Why the hours feel different at Level 2

The hour count is similar to Level I. The work is not.

Level I is 180 standalone multiple-choice questions. You read a question, you answer it, you move on. Level II is built entirely from **item sets**: 22 vignettes — one-to-two-page case scenarios — each followed by four multiple-choice questions, for **88 questions total**. Twenty of the 22 item sets are scored; two are unscored trial sets that you have no way of identifying.

The exam runs **4 hours and 24 minutes**, split into two equal sessions of 2 hours and 12 minutes with an optional break between them. Eleven item sets per session.

The practical consequence is that you can't answer a Level II question without first extracting the right three numbers from a page of narrative — and the vignette will contain numbers you don't need, deliberately. That's a different skill from recall, and it's the reason a candidate who "knows the material" can still run out of time. Roughly six minutes per item set, including reading the vignette, is the pace you're training for.

It's also why raw reading hours convert poorly into Level II readiness. Reading the curriculum builds knowledge; only timed vignettes build the extraction-and-pace skill the exam actually scores.

## A realistic 350-hour budget

Here's how the hours tend to divide for candidates who pass, working backward from a six-month runway at roughly 14 hours a week:

**First pass through the curriculum — about 180 hours.** Ten topics, and the weights tell you where the hours go. The 2026 Level II topic weights are: Financial Statement Analysis 10–15%, Equities 10–15%, Fixed Income 10–15%, Portfolio Construction 10–15%, Ethical and Professional Standards 10–15%, then Quantitative Methods, Economics, Corporate Finance, Derivatives and Risk Management, and Alternative Investments at 5–10% each.

Notice there is no 20% topic the way FSA once dominated. Level II is flatter than candidates expect, which means the "I'll skip a small topic" strategy is worse here than at Level I — five topics at 5–10% is a quarter of the exam.

**Second pass and problem work — about 100 hours.** This is where most of the learning actually happens. End-of-chapter problems, then item sets by topic. If your first pass took longer than 180 hours and you're eating into this block, you're making the classic Level II mistake: over-reading and under-practicing.

**Mock exams and review — about 70 hours.** Three to five full-length timed mocks, each with a review session that takes longer than the mock itself. A 4.5-hour mock plus a 5-hour honest review is a 10-hour weekend. That math is why mocks need to be scheduled in from the start, not squeezed into the last two weeks.

**Plus the Practical Skills Module.** Separate from the exam and easy to forget: you must complete one PSM at each level to receive your exam result, and it has to be a *different* module from the one you did at Level I. Budget a few evenings for it and do it early — candidates who leave it until exam month are adding stress they didn't need.

## What the pass rates say about the hours

Of the 18,111 candidates who took Level II in the May 2026 window, 43% passed. In the November 2025 window, CFA Institute reported a 50% pass rate for first-time candidates versus 27% for candidates testing after at least one deferral.

That gap is the most useful number in this article. Deferring is not neutral — it correlates with a pass rate roughly half that of candidates who take the exam as originally scheduled. Some of that is selection effect, but a lot of it is the retention problem: momentum lost is genuinely expensive at this level. If you're deciding between registering for a window you can just about prepare for and pushing to the next one, the data leans toward taking it on schedule with a compressed plan rather than deferring and restarting cold.

Level II runs in May, August, and November, so the windows are close enough together that a deferral rarely buys as much time as it feels like it will.

## How to know if your hours are working

Hours logged is a bad metric. Two better ones:

**Timed item-set accuracy by topic.** Not "did I understand the reading" but "can I get 3 of 4 right in six minutes without looking anything up." Track it per topic and you'll find your real weak spots are rarely the ones you assumed.

**Whether your mock score is moving.** If you've done 250 hours and your mock scores are flat, more reading hours won't fix it — the problem is almost always that you're studying passively. Switch the ratio to 70% problems, 30% reading and the curve usually starts moving within two weeks.
