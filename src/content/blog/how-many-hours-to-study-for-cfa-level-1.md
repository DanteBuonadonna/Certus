# How Many Hours to Study for CFA Level 1?

Plan on at least 300 hours for CFA Level 1 — that's the minimum CFA Institute recommends, and candidates who actually pass typically report somewhere in the 300–400 hour range. Over a standard six-month prep window, that works out to roughly 12–15 hours per week, every week, with no long breaks.

That's the short answer. The more useful question is *where those hours should go*, because two candidates can each log 350 hours and get completely different results. One spends 300 of them passively re-reading the curriculum; the other spends half their time answering practice questions under exam conditions. The second candidate passes far more often. Recent pass rates make the stakes clear: 43% of Level I candidates passed in November 2025, 45% in February 2026, and just 39% in May 2026 — against a 10-year average around 41%. More than half of candidates fail, and almost none of them fail because they studied zero hours. They fail because their hours were low-quality.

## Why 300 hours is the floor, not the target

The 300-hour figure is a minimum, and it assumes reasonably efficient studying. The 2026 Level 1 curriculum spans 10 topic areas, roughly 93 learning modules, and 365 learning outcome statements — all tested through 180 multiple-choice questions across two 135-minute sessions.

Whether you personally need 300 hours or 450 depends mostly on two things:

**Your background.** If you majored in finance or accounting, Financial Statement Analysis and Corporate Issuers will feel like review, and you might get away with the low end of the range. If you're coming from engineering, liberal arts, or a non-finance career, budget extra time — especially for FSA and Fixed Income, which have the steepest learning curves for newcomers.

**Your practice-to-reading ratio.** Hours spent answering questions and reviewing wrong answers are worth far more than hours spent reading. A common trap: candidates spend four months "getting through the curriculum" and leave three weeks for practice. Flip that. Aim to be doing practice questions from month one, alongside the reading.

## How to budget your hours by topic

Not all topics deserve equal time, because they're not weighted equally on the exam. Ethical and Professional Standards is the single heaviest topic at 15–20% of the exam. Financial Statement Analysis, Equity Investments, and Fixed Income sit in the 11–14% band each. Together, those four topics are roughly half your exam.

A sensible rough allocation of a 350-hour budget looks like this: give Ethics, FSA, Fixed Income, and Equity about 160–180 hours combined; give Quantitative Methods, Economics, and Derivatives about 90–100 hours; and cover Corporate Issuers, Alternative Investments, and Portfolio Management with the remaining 70–90. Then reserve your final four to six weeks almost entirely for mock exams and targeted review of your weak areas — that final stretch is where passes are actually earned.

One warning: don't "bank" Ethics for the end because it seems like common sense. It isn't. The Standards have precise, testable distinctions (what you *must* do versus what's *recommended*), and Ethics can tip a borderline result. Touch it early, then re-review it in your final two weeks when it's fresh.

## Sample timelines

**Six months (the standard path):** ~12–15 hours per week. Two hours on weekday evenings, longer blocks on weekends. This is the most forgiving schedule — it leaves room for a bad week without derailing you. Our [CFA Level 1 study guide](/blog/cfa-level-1-study-guide) walks through a full week-by-week structure.

**Four months:** ~20 hours per week. Doable, but only if you start practice questions immediately and skip the trap of reading everything twice.

**Three months:** ~25+ hours per week. Only realistic with a finance background or a very flexible schedule. At this pace, question banks and mocks *are* the study plan; there's no time for leisurely reading.

Whatever timeline you pick, work backward from your exam date and put the hours on your actual calendar. "I'll study 15 hours a week" fails; "Tuesday and Thursday 7–9pm, Saturday 9–1" survives contact with real life.

## Track hours, but measure practice scores

A final piece of hard-won advice: logging hours feels productive, but hours are an input, not an outcome. The metric that actually predicts passing is your score on timed practice, trending upward. If you're at 200 hours and scoring 55% on mocks, you don't need "100 more hours" — you need to find the specific topics dragging you down and drill them.
