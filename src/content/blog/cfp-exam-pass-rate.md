# CFP Exam Pass Rate: What the Numbers Really Say

The CFP® exam pass rate was **67%** for the March 2026 administration — 2,927 of 4,391 candidates passed. Over the last several years the overall rate has sat in a tight band between 62% and 68%, so 67% is a normal result, not a sign the exam got easier.

But the overall number is the least useful one CFP Board publishes. The split underneath it is where the actual lesson is.

## First-timers vs. repeaters

CFP Board reports pass rates separately for first-time takers and repeaters, and the gap is consistent enough to be a genuine finding rather than noise:

| Administration | Overall | First-time | Repeaters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| March 2026 | 67% | 69% | 60% |
| November 2025 | 64% | 65% | 58% |
| July 2025 | 64% | 68% | 56% |
| March 2025 | 65% | 68% | 56% |
| November 2024 | 62% | 66% | 50% |
| July 2024 | 65% | 69% | 50% |
| March 2024 | 68% | 72% | 54% |

Read that right column again. In four of the last seven administrations, **fewer than 60% of repeaters passed** — and in two of them it was an even coin flip.

This is counterintuitive. Repeaters have seen the real exam. They know the format, the pacing, the question style. They've already done the coursework. On paper they should outperform people walking in cold. They don't, and it isn't close.

## Why the retake is harder than it looks

The comfortable explanation is that repeaters are simply the candidates who were weakest to begin with — the bottom of the first-time distribution, re-pooled. That's part of it, and it's real selection bias.

But it isn't the whole story, because the gap is too wide and too stable. A more useful reading: **the second attempt fails for the same reason the first one did, and most candidates don't change anything structural between them.**

The default retake plan is "review my weak areas and take it again in four months." That plan quietly assumes the problem was knowledge gaps. Often it wasn't. It was one of these:

- **Application, not recall.** The CFP exam leans heavily on case-based, integrated questions. You can know every rule about Roth conversions and still miss a question that buries the conversion inside a client's estate plan and tax bracket. Rereading the material tests recall. The exam tests transfer.
- **Retrieval never got practiced.** Highlighting and re-watching feel productive because the material feels familiar. Familiarity is not retrievability. If your prep is mostly input, you find out the difference on exam day.
- **Endurance.** It's a long, dense exam. Judgment degrades in the back half, and nobody discovers their fade point by reading.

If the first attempt failed on application, doing more of the same input-heavy review is running the identical experiment and expecting a different result. That's what the repeater column is measuring.

## The stakes on a retake are higher than most candidates realize

Three constraints make "just take it again" a worse plan than it sounds.

**Attempts are capped.** CFP Board limits candidates to **five lifetime attempts**, and no more than **three within any 24-month period**. This is not an exam you can grind indefinitely.

**It's expensive to repeat.** Standard registration is **$925**. Early bird is **$825** (until six weeks before the registration deadline) and late registration is **$1,025** during the final week. Every retake is close to a thousand dollars.

**The calendar is unforgiving.** The exam runs only three times a year, in 8-day windows in March, July and November. A failed attempt costs you roughly four months, not four weeks. The next windows are Oct. 29 – Nov. 5, 2026, then March 16 – 23, 2027, and July 13 – 20, 2027 — and each has an education verification deadline weeks beforehand, so a missed deadline costs a full cycle.

Five attempts, ~$925 each, four months apart. That math is the argument for making the first attempt count.

## What the data actually recommends

The honest takeaway from the first-time/repeater split isn't "the exam is hard." It's that **how you prepare matters more than how long you prepare**, and the failure mode is well-documented.

Practically:

**Practice retrieval, not review.** Do questions before you feel ready. The discomfort of not knowing is the signal that learning is happening — the smoothness of rereading is the signal that it isn't.

**Work full cases, not isolated facts.** Since the exam integrates topics, prep that keeps topics in separate silos leaves the exact skill untested.

**Take at least one full-length timed run before exam day.** Not to score it. To find where your accuracy falls off, and to make the length itself unsurprising.

**If you're retaking, diagnose before you study.** CFP Board gives failed candidates performance feedback by domain. Use it to decide whether your problem was coverage or application — and if it was application, change the method, not just the hours.

## A note on comparing exams

CFP's ~65% is often set against CFA Level I's much lower rate as evidence the CFP is "easier." That comparison doesn't survive contact with the details: the candidate pools, eligibility requirements, and prerequisite coursework are completely different. The CFP exam sits behind an education requirement most candidates have already completed, which lifts the pass rate for reasons that have nothing to do with difficulty.
