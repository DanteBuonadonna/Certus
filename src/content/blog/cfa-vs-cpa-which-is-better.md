# CFA vs CPA: Which Is Better?

Short answer: the CFA charter is better if you want a career in investments — research, portfolio management, wealth management, or capital markets. The CPA is better if you want a career in accounting — audit, tax, financial reporting, or a path to CFO. Neither is "harder" in a way that should decide it for you; they're hard in different ways, and picking the one that matches the job you actually want matters far more than prestige comparisons.

Here's the honest breakdown on cost, time, difficulty, and payoff, using current numbers.

## What each credential actually is

The **CFA (Chartered Financial Analyst)** charter is awarded by CFA Institute after you pass three sequential exams (Levels I, II, and III) and complete 4,000 hours of qualifying work experience. The curriculum covers ethics, quantitative methods, economics, financial statement analysis, equity, fixed income, derivatives, alternatives, and portfolio management. It's the standard credential for investment analysis and portfolio management roles.

The **CPA (Certified Public Accountant)** license is granted by state boards of accountancy after you pass a four-section exam and meet education and experience requirements. Since the 2024 CPA Evolution overhaul, everyone takes three core sections — FAR (financial accounting), AUD (audit), and REG (regulation/tax) — plus one discipline section of your choice (BAR, ISC, or TCP). It's the required license for signing audit opinions and the default credential for serious accounting careers.

One structural difference matters a lot: the CPA is a **license** with legal authority — you cannot sign an audit opinion without it. The CFA is a **designation** — a strong market signal, but no role legally requires it.

## Cost: CPA is cheaper to take, but watch the education requirement

**CFA:** As of the February 2026 exam cycle, CFA Institute eliminated its one-time enrollment fee and folded it into registration. Level I and II now run $1,140 (early registration) to $1,490 (standard), and Level III runs $1,240 to $1,590. Pass all three on the first try with early registration and you're looking at roughly $3,520 in exam fees alone — more with standard deadlines or retakes.

**CPA:** NASBA's recommended exam fee is roughly $262–269 per section, so about $1,050–1,075 for all four, plus state application and registration fees that typically add another $100–350 depending on your jurisdiction. Call it $1,100–1,400 total in exam costs.

But the CPA has a hidden cost the CFA doesn't: education requirements. The traditional path requires 150 college credit hours — often a fifth year of school. That's changing fast: as of mid-2026, roughly two dozen jurisdictions have adopted an alternative pathway that accepts a bachelor's degree plus additional work experience instead of the 150 hours, with more states phasing it in through 2027. If you're weighing the CPA, check your specific state board before assuming you need the extra year.

Neither figure includes prep courses, which is where costs really balloon — big-name CFA prep runs $350–$1,500 per level. (That's the problem [Certus](/cheapest-cfa-prep) exists to fix.)

## Difficulty and pass rates: both are hard, differently

**CFA pass rates** are famously low. Level I came in at 43% in the November 2025 window and 39% in May 2026, against a 10-year average of about 41%. The exams are sequential — you can't touch Level II until you pass Level I — so one exam per window is your maximum pace, and the full program typically takes 3–4 years.

**CPA pass rates** vary sharply by section. In 2025, FAR ran about 42%, AUD about 48%, and REG above 60%. Unlike the CFA, you take the four sections in any order, year-round, so most candidates finish the exam itself in 12–18 months.

Study time: CFA candidates report averaging roughly 300 hours per level — 900+ hours across the program. CPA estimates vary widely by source, but most review providers suggest around 100–150 hours per section, or 400–600 hours total. The CFA is the longer grind; the CPA is the faster sprint with more scheduling flexibility.

## Salary and career payoff

Averages are close enough that this shouldn't drive your decision: recent U.S. figures put average CPA salaries around $88K and CFA charterholders around $95K, with both ranging much higher at senior levels. The real difference is the *shape* of each career.

CPA careers are structurally protected — audits legally require CPAs, every company needs accountants, and demand is stable even in downturns. The path is well-mapped: public accounting, then senior, manager, controller, CFO.

CFA careers have higher variance. Investment roles pay more at the top — portfolio managers and senior analysts can clear several multiples of the averages — but the industry is more competitive, more cyclical, and the charter alone doesn't guarantee entry. It's a strong differentiator, not a golden ticket.

## Can you do both?

Some people do, usually CPAs moving into equity research or corporate finance who add the charter later. The combination is powerful for roles that sit at the intersection — valuation, forensic finance, investment roles covering financials. But for most people, doubling up is overkill. Pick the one that matches your target job.

## How to decide

Ask one question: **do you want to analyze investments, or account for businesses?**

Choose the **CFA** if you're aiming at asset management, equity research, wealth management, hedge funds, or capital markets — or if you're an accountant trying to pivot into investments.

Choose the **CPA** if you're aiming at public accounting, audit, tax, controllership, or a CFO track — or if you want the most recession-resistant credential in finance.

Choose **neither** (yet) if you're unsure. The CFA Level I curriculum is an excellent broad finance education either way, and you can test your footing cheaply before committing thousands of dollars.
